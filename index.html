The PIFRA Vendor Number: How the Government Pays Its Suppliers

Employees are paid against a personnel number. Suppliers are paid against a vendor number — and half the stuck purchase bills in any office are stuck on exactly that.

Every government office buys things: stationery, fuel, furniture, repairs, printing, refreshments for official meetings. None of that money moves on a name and a handshake. In the same financial system that runs payroll — the one your PIFRA registration plugs you into as an employee — every supplier exists as a record with its own identifier: the vendor number. If you have ever processed a purchase bill, or waited while one crawled through the accounts office, this number was somewhere in the story.

What a vendor number actually is

It is the supplier's identity in the government payment system: one unique number for one firm or individual, created once and reused on every bill that supplier ever submits to any office on the system. Attached to that number sits the record that makes payment possible — the firm's name and address, its tax numbers (NTN and, where applicable, sales tax registration), and the bank account into which government payments are to be credited.

The parallel with employees is exact. You are paid because a personnel record exists, carries your account details, and is attached to a DDO who can draw your pay. A supplier is paid because a vendor record exists, carries their account details, and can be named on a bill drawn by that same DDO.

Why an employee should care about a supplier's number

Because in most offices, employees are the ones moving purchase files. The dealing assistant prepares the bill, the DDO signs it, and the accounts office pays it — but a bill cannot even be properly created until the supplier's vendor number is quoted on it. Three everyday situations follow:

  • The new supplier. Your office buys from a firm that has never supplied the government before. There is no vendor number yet, so before the first payment can happen, the firm must be registered in the system — a step nobody budgeted time for, discovered at the worst moment.
  • The wrong number. A digit copied wrongly from an old bill points the payment machinery at a different firm, or at nobody. The bill comes back objected to, and the file starts its journey again.
  • The dormant record. A supplier who has not been paid in years may have a record that needs reactivation or updated bank details before a fresh payment will go through — banks close and change accounts; system records do not update themselves.

What the record has to get right

A vendor payment fails quietly at the last stage when the record and reality disagree. The details that matter most are the ones nobody re-checks: the bank account and IBAN (payments are credited directly, so a stale account means a bounced payment), the tax status (withholding on a supplier's bill depends on whether the firm is an active taxpayer, and the deduction is calculated from the record, not from what the firm claims on the day), and the exact registered name, which should match the firm's tax record and its invoices. A mismatch in any of the three is enough to hold a bill at audit.

How a supplier gets a number

Registration runs through the accounts office, on the strength of the firm's documents: tax registration, bank account proof, and the business's basic particulars. Once created, the number is not office-specific — the same vendor number serves the firm across offices on the system, which is why an experienced government supplier quotes it on their very first invoice and why a firm that supplies several departments guards the record's accuracy carefully.

The office habit that prevents most of this

Check the vendor before the purchase, not after the bill. Confirm the number exists, that the bank details on the record are the ones on the supplier's current cheque or account letter, and that the name matches the invoice the firm will actually submit. Five minutes at the file's start saves the three-week return journey that a wrong vendor number guarantees — the bill objected to at audit, sent back to the office, corrected, resubmitted, and queued all over again behind everyone else's.
